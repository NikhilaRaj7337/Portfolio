Stacked Ensembles and Species Embeddings for Caribbean Forest Height Modelling

My MS thesis at the University of Georgia: a comparison of Random Forest, XGBoost, an MLP with learned species embeddings and a Ridge stacking ensemble on 42,158 trees from Puerto Rico and the U.S. Virgin Islands.

AdvisorsDr. Sheng-I Yang, Dr. Frederick Maier
CommitteeDr. Pete Bettinger
DegreeMS, University of Georgia
ApprovedAugust 2026
On this page ProblemData & splitModelsResultsSpeciesEnsembleLimits

The problem

Foresters measure diameter at breast height (DBH) on every tree because it's fast and cheap. Height needs special equipment, so it's measured on only a sample. Height-diameter (H-D) models fill the gap, and their predictions feed volume, biomass and carbon estimates.

Caribbean forests make this hard. The data covers 409 species, many with only a handful of measurements, and stands range from dry scrub to montane cloud forest and are regularly reset by hurricanes. No single fixed equation fits. I treated H-D as a supervised regression problem and asked two questions: how well can modern ML models predict height here, and which predictors matter most?

Data and a leakage-free split

Trees42,158USDA Forest Inventory & Analysis measurements
Plots474Puerto Rico and U.S. Virgin Islands
Species409354 kept, rare and unseen pooled as "Others"

Predictors were tree-level (DBH, a distance-independent competition index) and stand-level (trees per hectare, Shannon species diversity, forest type, island, disturbance).

Trees in the same plot share conditions, so a random tree-level split leaks information. I split by plot instead: 379 plots for training and 95 for testing, with no overlap. All tuning used training plots only, and predictors were normalized on training statistics.

Four models, one protocol

  • Random Forest: bagged regression trees with two-stage tuning.
  • XGBoost: regularized gradient boosting with early stopping.
  • MLP with species embeddings: each species gets a learned 8-dimensional vector, concatenated with the covariates. Rare species' vectors shrink toward the mean during training, so they need no manual calibration.
  • Stacking ensemble: a Ridge regression meta-learner over 5-fold, plot-level out-of-fold predictions from the three base models. Its fitted intercept corrects bias they share.

An ordinary least-squares model on DBH served as the naive baseline.

The ensemble had the lowest test error, near-zero bias and by far the smallest train-test gap. Its 2.126 m falls within the 2.0–2.3 m range reported for the mixed-effects Random Forest benchmark of Yang et al. (2022), here under a stricter plot-level split. Every learned model beat the baseline, and DBH and competition index were the dominant predictors.

All models did better on the Virgin Islands (ensemble 1.92 m) than on Puerto Rico (2.18 m), whose forests range from mangroves to montane rainforest.

For named species, the embedding MLP was the best single model. For species never seen in training, every model failed, and embeddings did worse than plain integer coding. I report that as a negative result. Embeddings learn well from data and can't invent a species they never saw.

Why the ensemble works

The Ridge meta-learner gave XGBoost 47.2% of the weight, the MLP 38.8% and Random Forest 13.9%, roughly tracking each model's out-of-fold error. XGBoost and the MLP had similar error but biases in opposite directions, so combining them cancels some of it.

All three base models under-predicted tall trees. The meta-learner's fitted intercept absorbed that shared bias, bringing the ensemble's test bias to −0.034 m, with the largest benefit for canopy trees over 20 m.

Limitations and future work

  • No climate or terrain predictors; adding WorldClim bioclimatic and terrain features is the obvious next step.
  • Unseen species remain the weak point; few-shot or hierarchical (genus-level) embeddings could help.
  • The ensemble uses fixed weights, so it can't adapt to species-specific error; a context-aware meta-learner could.
  • Other directions: LiDAR or GEDI canopy-height covariates, transfer to Trinidad and Tobago, and prediction intervals via quantile regression or conformal prediction.
